First part conclusion

It follows that the more the correlationist intends to preserve the internal identity of each theory, the less he can sustain a strong integration between them; and, conversely, the more he intends to structurally integrate their elements, the more he must admit that at least one of the theories ceases to preserve its own determinations in their entirety. Correlation, therefore, cannot remain neutral in both directions simultaneously. If each theory preserves its own language, its internal relations, its criteria of succession, and the particular manner in which it apprehends the object, then the most that may be established between them is a comparative or analogical relation. In such a case, correlation derives similarities, but it does not produce a common structure capable of legitimately modifying the original terms. If, on the other hand, something beyond this is intended — that is, that one theory determine, restrict, correct, or alter elements of another — then we are no longer dealing with mere correlation, but with the introduction of a rule of dependence between systems. And this rule must be demonstrated independently of the mere identity of the object studied, because it is precisely this rule that transforms comparison into interference. An unavoidable asymmetry therefore arises: either the theories remain independent and the correlation is merely derivative, or one of them begins to exercise a normative function over the other, in which case the supposed neutrality of integration disappears. There does not appear to be a third path in which both remain entirely autonomous while, simultaneously, one is capable of altering the internal results of the other without the introduction of some external rule. The problem, therefore, is no longer simply whether two theories may be correlated, but what kind of relation this correlation purports to establish. If it merely recognizes similarities, then it alters nothing. If it restricts possibilities, then it must justify both the direction and the criterion of that restriction. If it transforms internal determinations, then it must demonstrate the principle according to which one theory acquires constitutive power over the other. The question therefore becomes less “which type corresponds to which type?” and more “what is the logical nature of the correspondence itself?” Until this has been established, every correlational result remains secondary
